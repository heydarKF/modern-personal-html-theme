Why I Left Frameworks Behind.

This is not a framework-bashing essay. Frameworks are fine. This is about what happens when you stop renting your architecture and finally buy it.

For six years I shipped on frameworks. They were good rooms: furnished, lit, someone else paying for the plumbing. Then a client asked for a change that cut across three of the framework’s opinions, and I spent two days fighting furniture I was not allowed to move.

01The rent comes due

Every abstraction is a contract with a stranger’s future decisions. Upgrades arrive as ultimatums. Magic saves time until the day it costs a week. I counted the hours I spent working around my tools versus working with them, and the ledger was embarrassing.

I didn’t want fewer features. I wanted fewer surprises.

The turning point was not ideological. It was arithmetic: a maintenance quote that was mostly archaeology, and a upgrade path that required rewriting code the framework had told me never to touch. When the tool’s roadmap becomes your roadmap, you are no longer the author of your system.[1]

02What pure MVC actually buys

A router I can read in one sitting. A container with forty lines, not four thousand. Middleware that is just functions. When something breaks at 2am, the stack trace ends in code I wrote, which means it ends in code I can fix.

app/Core/Router.php
// the whole magic
final class Router {
  public function dispatch(Request $r): Response {
    $handler = $this->match($r->method(), $r->path());
    return $this->pipeline($r, $handler);
  }
}

Speed came too — 3× less memory, no boot cache to invalidate — but that was the side effect. The product was ownership: every line auditable, every decision mine to defend.

Measured, not vibes: on the same workload, the pure-MVC rewrite served p95 at 94ms against 210ms on the framework build, with resident memory down from 148MB to 41MB. Numbers from Ledgerly’s hardening sprint.

03The honest caveat

You pay for this in discipline. No framework means no one else enforcing structure; your own standards become the only guardrail. If your team can’t hold a convention without a compiler’s help, rent the room. It’s a fine room.

  • Write the conventions down before the first controller, not after the tenth.
  • Make the container strict: unknown ids throw at boot with the resolution chain printed.
  • Keep a performance gate beside the tests, or “fast” becomes a story you tell.
  • Budget review time for the core itself — it is now your product too.

But if you can hold it, buying the house changes how you sleep. The next essay in this shelf goes deeper on the smallest piece of that house: the forty-line container.

04What I kept from the frameworks

Leaving is not looting. The good ideas came with me, stripped of their ceremony:

  1. Middleware pipelines — as plain closures composing around a handler.
  2. Request DTOs — validation at the boundary, typed objects downstream.
  3. Service providers — as one explicit bindings file you can read aloud.
  4. Migrations — as numbered SQL with a runner that refuses to skip.

Frameworks are libraries of hard-won decisions. You can adopt the decisions without adopting the dependency. That is the whole trick, and it is not a trick at all — it is reading.
